## Official submission signals

Adobe’s current contributor upload guidelines state that photos use JPEG and require a minimum of 4MP; Adobe’s photo overview highlights real people in natural environments, authentic everyday relationships, remote work/technology/industries, sustainable living/climate action, cultural heritage/holidays/traditions, and healthcare/wellness/mental health as popular subject areas. Adobe also emphasizes useful, diverse, high-quality photos and warns against repetitive or outdated submissions. These are platform guidance signals, not a guarantee of demand or sales.

Shutterstock’s current photo submission guide states that JPEG and TIFF are accepted, images must be at least 4MP, files under 50MB can be uploaded directly, titles/descriptions plus 7–50 keywords and at least one category are required, and releases/editorial designations may be needed. Shutterstock also exposes machine-generated keyword suggestions that contributors should select/review rather than blindly accept. Submission and approval remain human/platform-controlled.

Freepik’s contributor FAQ says useful content, awareness of current trends and user demand, and accurate tags/titles matter for downloads and review. It explicitly warns that poorly tagged resources may be rejected and points contributors toward technical, composition, aesthetic, and similar-submission guidance. This is broad contributor guidance and is older than the current Adobe/Shutterstock pages, so it should be treated as a supporting source.

## Immediate JPEG implications

The JPEG route should optimize for useful visual stories and distinct commercial contexts rather than generic attractive scenes. Candidate families should be evaluated against buyer job, current relevance, diversity/authenticity, thumbnail clarity, copy-space utility when appropriate, legal/release burden, and saturation. The existing technical pipeline already protects dimensions, RGB/sRGB and file-size constraints, but it does not itself prove visual quality, originality, legal clearance, or market demand.

## Adobe technical, quality, and GenAI requirements — verified active URLs

The active Adobe technical/legal photo page states: 4–100MP, maximum 45MB, JPEG with sRGB color profile, sharp focus and good lighting, no watermarks/timestamps/branding, and model/property releases where recognizable people, identifiable locations, artwork, or other protected property require them. The previously guessed URL ending in `technical-legal-requirements-for-photo-submissions.html` returned 404 and must not be used as an active source.

Adobe’s active quality/refusal guidance identifies over/underexposure, softness/out-of-focus, poor white balance/color correction, heavy editing or oversharpening, dust/sensor spots, unnatural saturation, chromatic aberration, noise, artifacts, and similar/repetitive submissions as risks. Adobe advises reviewing at 100%, retaining an original backup, using subtle color/noise/sharpness correction, maintaining authentic texture, and submitting only the strongest distinct images rather than many near-duplicates. These are the most important JPEG QA opportunities for StockForge beyond the existing dimension/color/size gate.

Adobe’s active Generative AI submission page requires commercial-use rights from the AI tool, accurate title/keywords without artist names, real people, fictional characters, copyrighted works, government agencies, or technical prompt parameters, and selecting the Created using generative AI checkbox. Recognizable real people/property require the appropriate releases; fully generative people/property use the fictional checkbox. AI output must be checked for anatomy, proportions, lighting, and intentional visual elements, and only unique-value iterations should be submitted.

## JPEG maturation implications

StockForge’s current deterministic gate covers the objective Adobe baseline: JPEG format, RGB pixel mode, 4–100MP dimensions, file size up to 45MB, embedded sRGB profile, and safe decodability. It does not yet prove sharpness at 100%, natural lighting, realistic anatomy/object geometry, absence of chromatic aberration, dust, AI artifacts, distinctness, legal clearance, or buyer usefulness. Those should remain separate review gates rather than being falsely collapsed into the technical PASS.

## Adobe category evidence

Adobe’s active category guide explains that categories help customers filter content and recommends using the category that matches the content’s theme, context, mood, and intent rather than choosing the most popular option. The current list includes Business, The Environment, Food, Graphic Resources, Industry, Lifestyle, People, Science, Technology, and others. Adobe Sensei may suggest a category, but contributors are expected to review and correct it. StockForge should therefore keep automatic category mapping conservative and require explicit human review where a scene crosses categories or where the lane is not a clear match.

## Global visual trend signals

Adobe’s 2026 Creative Trends report describes four broad creative directions: All the Feels (sensory, tactile, bold visuals), Connectioneering (authentic emotional connection), Surreal Silliness (unexpected playful concepts), and Local Flavor (regional specificity and cultural authenticity). Adobe states that the report combines internal/external research, Creative Cloud community feedback, commercial campaigns, and search-history analysis; it is a trend forecast, not a guarantee that a particular stock image will sell.[6] [7]

Envato’s 2026 photography trend article describes AI-assisted editing, cinematic photography, candid/authentic photography, mobile-first framing, drone/aerial views, motion blur, analog/vintage aesthetics, age-positive casting, messy aesthetics, and screenshot culture. These are editorial trend signals from a marketplace education channel. Some are unsuitable or risky for Adobe Stock without careful filtering: screenshot culture may involve UI/text/third-party IP; celebrity/brand references and recognizable properties require rights review; heavy blur, noise, grading, or AI artifacts can conflict with Adobe quality guidance. The safe use is to treat trend signals as concept inputs, then filter them through buyer job, legal risk, distinctness, technical quality, and platform policy.[8]
